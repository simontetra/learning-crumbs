Records and tapes store sound as wobbles: in a groove, or in magnetism on a tape. A **compact disc** stores it as **numbers**. Millions of microscopic pits are pressed into a shiny disc, and a tiny **laser** reads them as it spins. No needle ever touches it, so it doesn't wear out.

Two companies built the CD together: **Philips** in the Netherlands and **Sony** in Japan. Philips' first design was 11.5 centimetres across and held about **an hour** of music.

Sony's president, **Norio Ohga**, a trained opera singer, thought that was too little. He wanted a disc that could hold a whole opera, or all of **Beethoven's Ninth Symphony**. The final disc grew to **12 centimetres**, holding **74 minutes**.

You will often hear that the CD was made exactly the length of Beethoven's Ninth. The truth is fuzzier: the stories disagree about whose idea it was, and most recordings of the Ninth are a few minutes shorter. It was one of the pieces Sony wanted to fit, not a measuring stick.

The first CD players went on sale in Japan on **1 October 1982**. The first pop album sold on CD was Billy Joel's *52nd Street*.

> **Look closer:** Shine a light on the shiny side of a CD and you'll see a rainbow. The rows of tiny pits split white light into its colours.
