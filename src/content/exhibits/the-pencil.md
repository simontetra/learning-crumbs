Sometime in the 1500s, near **Seathwaite** in the valley of **Borrowdale**, in what is now the Lake District, people found a deposit of something remarkable: very pure, solid **graphite**. It was soft, dark and slippery, and it left a clean grey mark on whatever it touched. Local farmers used it to mark their sheep.

Nobody then knew what graphite was. It looked like a kind of metal **lead**, so people called it *plumbago* or "black lead". The name stuck. To this day we talk about the lead in a pencil, even though pencils have never contained any.

Pure graphite sticks could be sawn from the Borrowdale lumps, wrapped in string, and later fitted snugly into grooves in wood. Artists loved them. Unlike a quill, a pencil needed no ink, and its marks could be rubbed away.

Then came a war. In **1795**, with France cut off from English graphite, a French inventor called **Nicolas-Jacques Conté** found a new way: mix powdered graphite with **clay**, shape it into rods and bake them. More clay made a harder, paler pencil; less made a softer, darker one. That is still how pencils are made, and why they come in grades like H and B.

> **Look closer:** In 1832 the first pencil factory in Britain opened in **Keswick**, near where the graphite was found. The town still makes pencils, and has a pencil museum.
