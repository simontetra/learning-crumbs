About 2,000 years ago, the Roman writer **Pliny the Elder** told a story about how glass was discovered. Some sailors carrying a cargo of **natron**, a kind of natural soda, landed on a sandy beach to cook a meal. They couldn't find stones to rest their pot on, so they used lumps of natron from the ship. When the fire was hot, the soda and the sand beneath it melted together and ran out as a stream of a strange new liquid that hardened into **glass**.

It is a lovely story, and it gets the ingredients right: sand, soda and fire. But it is probably not how glass began. By Pliny's time, people had already been making glass for a very long time.

Nobody knows who made the first glass. Experts at the Corning Museum of Glass think true glass was probably first made in western Asia, perhaps **Mesopotamia**, at least **4,000 years ago**. Before that, people had made a glazed material called faience for over a thousand years.

Glassmakers guarded their methods. Some of the oldest glass recipes were written down about **3,300 years ago**, pressed into **clay tablets** in cuneiform, one of the earliest kinds of writing, and kept secret.

> **Look closer:** Pliny's tale is like many origin stories in this museum: it may not be true, but it has been told for two thousand years because it is easy to picture.
