A pencil can be rubbed out. Ink is meant to stay. But what is it made of?

In **China**, the answer was **soot**. Ink-makers collected the fine black powder left by burning lamps or pine wood and mixed it with glue into hard **ink sticks**. A painter or writer rubbed the stick with a little water on a smooth stone to make fresh ink. Black ink was being used in China more than 3,000 years ago. In English it came to be called *India ink*, because of the trade routes it travelled along, not because it was made in India.

In Europe and the Middle East, the great ink came from an odd place: the **oak tree**. When a tiny **wasp** lays an egg in an oak bud, the tree grows a hard little ball around it, called a **gall**, which becomes a nursery for the young wasp. Oak galls are full of tannin. Crush them and soak them in water, stir in some **iron sulphate** and a sticky gum, and you have **iron gall ink**.

It does something magical. It goes on the page quite pale, then turns darker and darker as it meets the air. It is clearly written about from around **AD 420**, and for more than a thousand years it was the ink of quill pens. Leonardo da Vinci, Rembrandt, Bach and Van Gogh all used it.

It has a dark side too. Over centuries, iron gall ink can slowly eat into the paper beneath it, leaving letters that crack or even fall out. Museums and libraries now work hard to slow it down.

> **Look closer:** Dip a quill, write a word, and watch it darken as it dries. For about a thousand years, that was how most letters, maps and drawings in Europe were made.
