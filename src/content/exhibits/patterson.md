Rocks can be clocks. Some kinds of atoms slowly change into other kinds at a steady rate, so by measuring how much has changed, scientists can tell how long it has been ticking. Over billions of years, **uranium** slowly turns into **lead**.

But Earth's oldest rocks have been melted, squashed and recycled many times. So **Clair Patterson**, a young American scientist, looked somewhere else: at a **meteorite**, a lump of rock from space that formed at the same time as the Earth and has hardly changed since. He used a piece from the **Canyon Diablo** meteorite, which made Meteor Crater in Arizona.

There was a problem. Every time he measured lead, he got far too much. It was getting in from everywhere: the air, the dust, his equipment. So Patterson designed and built his own ultra-**clean laboratory**, and even re-purified his own chemicals. In **1956** he published his answer: the Earth is about **4.55 billion years old**. It's still the accepted figure today.

Then he asked: where was all that lead coming from? He found it in sea water, snow, air and people's bodies, and traced much of it to one source: **leaded petrol**, burned in car engines. He spent the rest of his career warning about it, even though not everyone wanted to hear. But leaded petrol was phased out, and the lead in people's blood fell by about half.

> **Look closer:** To answer the oldest question about the planet, he had to clean up a laboratory. And that made him notice the dirt in the air we all breathe.
