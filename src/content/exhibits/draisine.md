On **12 June 1817**, a German inventor called **Karl Drais** set off through the streets of Mannheim on a strange new machine. It had two wooden wheels, one behind the other, a seat and a handlebar for steering. It had **no pedals**. You sat astride it and pushed yourself along with your feet, like a child on a balance bike.

Drais called it his **Laufmaschine**, German for "running machine". It soon became known as the **draisine**, and in Britain as the **dandy horse** or **hobby horse**. Accounts of that first ride vary, but it was clearly quicker than walking.

Why then? A popular theory points to a volcano. In 1815 **Mount Tambora** in Indonesia erupted, throwing so much ash into the sky that 1816 became the **"Year Without a Summer"**. Crops failed across Europe, and with little feed, fewer horses could be kept. A machine that could do a horse's job without eating anything was suddenly very appealing. It is a good story, though nobody can be sure it was Drais's reason.

The craze didn't last long. Riders went too fast on pavements, and some cities banned them. But the idea of balancing on two wheels in a line had arrived.

> **Look closer:** Today's balance bikes for toddlers, with no pedals, work exactly like Drais's running machine. The first bicycle has quietly come back.
