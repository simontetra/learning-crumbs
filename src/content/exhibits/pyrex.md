Pour boiling water into a cold, ordinary glass and it may crack. Glass doesn't like sudden changes of temperature.

That was a real danger on the railways. Signal lanterns glowed hot inside, and when cold rain or icy air hit the glass, it could shatter, leaving a signal dark. So the Corning Glass Works in New York made a special tough glass for railway lanterns, called **Nonex**, that could take hot and cold without breaking.

In 1913 a young scientist called **Jesse Littleton** moved to Corning to work there. His wife, **Bessie**, had just had a casserole dish break in her oven. She asked him to bring her something made of the tough glass. He came home with the sawn-off bottoms of two **battery jars**, and she baked a **sponge cake** in one. It came out evenly cooked, and the glass was fine.

Corning's scientists took the idea seriously. After more experiments, by many people, the company began selling glass ovenware a few years later under the name **Pyrex**. By 1920 it was appearing in kitchens across America.

> **Look closer:** The Corning Museum of Glass calls the cake a "near-mythological" story, because the real history involved many people and experiments. But it was the moment that showed what the glass could do.
