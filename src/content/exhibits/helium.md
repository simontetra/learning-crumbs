In **1835**, the French philosopher **Auguste Comte** wrote that there were some things we could never find out. One of them was what the **stars** are made of. We could measure where they were and how they moved, he said, but we "shall never be able by any means to study their chemical composition".

He was wrong, and quite soon.

Scientists found that when you spread light out into a **rainbow**, with a prism, there are tiny dark or bright **lines** in it. In **1859**, two German scientists, **Robert Bunsen** and **Gustav Kirchhoff**, showed that each chemical element makes its own pattern of lines, like a fingerprint. The lines in sunlight matched elements we already knew, like sodium. Suddenly, you could tell what something was made of just from its light.

Then, during a total **eclipse** of the Sun in **August 1868**, astronomers looking at the Sun's edge saw a bright **yellow line** that didn't match anything known. The French astronomer **Pierre Janssen** saw it in India; the Englishman **Norman Lockyer** saw it too. Lockyer decided it must be a brand-new element, and named it after the Greek word for the Sun, *helios*: **helium**.

For **27 years**, helium was known only on the Sun. Then, in **1895**, the Scottish chemist **William Ramsay** found it on Earth, inside a rock.

> **Look closer:** The gas in a floating party balloon was discovered on the Sun before anyone found it here.
