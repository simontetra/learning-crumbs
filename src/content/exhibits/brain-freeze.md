Take a big, fast mouthful of ice cream or a slushy drink, and a few moments later: **ouch**. A sharp pain right in the front of your head.

Here is the strange part. Your **brain** itself can't feel pain at all. But the thin layers that wrap around it are full of nerves that can, and so are the blood vessels nearby.

Scientists are still arguing about exactly what happens. One idea is that the sudden cold on the **roof of your mouth** makes the blood vessels there squeeze narrow, then quickly open wide again, and the nerves report that rush as pain. Another is that the cold sets off a bundle of nerves at the back of the palate. Either way, your head gets the message even though it was your mouth that got cold.

The good news is that it doesn't last long, and you can help. Press your **tongue** against the roof of your mouth, or sip something warm, to warm it up again.

> **Look closer:** In 2002 a study in the *BMJ*, a medical journal, found that people who ate ice cream quickly got brain freeze more often. The simplest cure of all: slow down.
