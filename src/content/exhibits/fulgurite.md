People need a furnace to make glass. Nature only needs a thunderstorm.

When a bolt of **lightning** hits the ground, it can pour through damp sand in a fraction of a second. The heat is so intense that it melts the sand grains it passes through and fuses them together. When it cools, what's left in the ground is a **hollow tube of glass** that traces the path the lightning took, sometimes forking like the bolt itself.

These tubes are called **fulgurites**, from *fulgur*, the Latin word for lightning. People also call them **petrified lightning**. The outside is usually rough and crusted with sand grains that didn't quite melt. Inside, they can be smooth and glassy.

In **1812**, fulgurites were found in the sand hills at **Drigg**, on the coast of Cumbria. Three tubes were found close together, probably different forks of the same strike, and they ran several metres down into the ground. The Oxford University Museum of Natural History tells their story.

> **Look closer:** Fulgurites are fragile, a bit like a long straw of glass. Digging one out of the ground in one piece takes great care, and patience.
