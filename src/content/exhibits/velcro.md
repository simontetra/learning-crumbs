In **1941**, a Swiss engineer called **George de Mestral** went hunting in the **Jura mountains** with his dog. When they got home, both of them were covered in **burdock burrs**, the prickly seed heads that cling to anything that brushes past.

Most people would just pick them off. De Mestral put one under a **microscope**. He saw that each burr was covered in hundreds of tiny **hooks**, and that they caught in the little **loops** of thread in his clothes and in his dog's fur.

He wondered whether he could make a fastener that worked the same way: one strip covered in hooks, another covered in loops. It turned out to be very hard. It took him about **fourteen years**, with help from a weaver and a loom maker. In the end he found that **nylon**, sewn and treated in just the right way, made tough little hooks.

He named it **Velcro**, from two French words: *velours*, velvet, and *crochet*, hook. He applied for a patent in Switzerland in 1951, and patents followed in other countries by **1955**. Later, NASA used it on space missions, to stop things floating away.

> **Look closer:** Copying a clever idea from nature has a name: *biomimicry*. Velcro is one of its most famous examples.
