Ordinary glass breaks into sharp, dangerous pieces. So how can a car windscreen crack all over and still stay in one piece?

In **1903**, a French chemist and artist called **Édouard Bénédictus** knocked an empty glass flask off his shelf. He heard it smash, but when he picked it up it had kept its shape. It was cracked everywhere, yet hardly any pieces had fallen out.

The flask had once held a liquid plastic called **cellulose nitrate**. It had dried into a thin, clear film on the inside of the glass, and that film held all the broken pieces together.

Bénédictus realised what this could mean. He made a sandwich: a layer of clear plastic between two sheets of glass. In **1909** he patented it. Car makers were slow to use it at first, but during the First World War it was used for the eyepieces of gas masks.

Today almost every car windscreen is made the same way, as **laminated glass**: two layers of glass with a tough, clear plastic layer between them. If a stone hits it, it cracks into a spider's web, but it doesn't fall in on the people inside.

> **Look closer:** Many inventions in this museum started with an accident. The trick is to be the person who stops and asks *why*.
