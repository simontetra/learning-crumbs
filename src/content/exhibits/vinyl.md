Edison's recordings were wound around **cylinders**, a bit like a tin can on its side. They worked, but they were awkward to store and hard to copy.

In **1887** an inventor called **Emile Berliner** tried something different. Instead of a cylinder, he cut the sound into a **flat disc**, in one long groove that spirals from the edge towards the middle. A needle rides the groove as the disc spins, wobbling as it goes, and the wobbles become sound again. He called his machine the **gramophone**.

Flat discs could be stacked on a shelf, posted in an envelope, and pressed out by the thousand from a single master, like biscuits from a cutter. Soon nearly all music was sold that way.

There was a problem, though. For years records were made of **shellac**, which is brittle: drop one and it could break. And they spun fast, so each side held only a few minutes of music.

In **June 1948** the Columbia record company launched the **long-playing record**, or **LP**. It was made of **vinyl**, a plastic that bends instead of shattering, and turned slowly, **33⅓** times a minute, so a side could play for about 23 minutes. Columbia boasted it held around six times as much music as the old records. Whole symphonies, and later whole albums, could fit on one disc.

> **Look closer:** Vinyl records never quite went away. Many people still love the warm crackle and the big square cover you can hold.
