Here is a secret about words: sometimes nobody knows where they came from. *Raining cats and dogs* is one of those, and that is what makes it fun.

**What we do know.** In **1651** the Welsh poet **Henry Vaughan** wrote about a roof that kept out a downpour of "dogs and cats". A year later, a play by **Richard Brome** joked that "it shall rain dogs and polecats". In **1738**, **Jonathan Swift**, who wrote *Gulliver's Travels*, put the phrase in a book poking fun at silly, overused chatter, with a character afraid it would "rain cats and dogs". That may be how it caught on.

**The theories.**

- *Dead animals in the street.* In 1710 Swift wrote a poem about a London rainstorm so heavy that the floods swept dead cats and other rubbish along the streets. Perhaps people saw them after a storm.
- *Old myths.* In Norse stories, the storm god Odin was linked with dogs and wolves, signs of wind, and witches riding the storm were pictured with black cats.
- *A lost word.* There was once an English word **catadupe**, meaning a waterfall. Maybe "raining cats and dogs" began as "raining waterfalls", and the sound got muddled.

**The one that's wrong.** You may hear that in the old days cats and dogs slept in thatched roofs and slid off when it rained. Experts don't believe it. A good thatched roof sheds water, and an animal would have to be lying on the outside to fall off.

> **Look closer:** Swift was poking fun at phrases people repeated without thinking. Today we'd call them clichés, a word that began in a printer's workshop.
