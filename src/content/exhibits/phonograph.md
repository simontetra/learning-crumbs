Sound is a wobble in the air. Your voice makes it, your ears catch it, and a moment later it is gone. For almost all of history there was no way to keep it.

In late **1877**, **Thomas Edison** sketched a machine to try. A horn gathered the sound of a voice and made a tiny needle shiver. Under the needle, a cylinder wrapped in a thick sheet of **tinfoil** turned slowly, and the shivering needle pressed a wobbly groove into the foil.

Then came the clever part. Wind the cylinder back, let the needle run along the groove again, and the wobbles travel the other way: from the groove, through the needle, out of the horn. Edison spoke a nursery rhyme, *Mary had a little lamb*, and the machine said it back to him. Crackly and strange, but recognisably his own words.

He showed it to the magazine *Scientific American* on **7 December 1877**. People could hardly believe a machine could talk. He called it the **phonograph**, Greek for "sound writing".

> **Look closer:** Edison's was the first recording anyone could play back, but not the first recording. In 1860 a Frenchman, Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville, traced a song as squiggles on sooty paper. He never meant it to be heard. In 2008, scientists turned his squiggles back into sound. At first they played it too fast and thought it was a girl singing. Slowed to the right speed, it is almost certainly Scott himself, singing *Au clair de la lune*.
