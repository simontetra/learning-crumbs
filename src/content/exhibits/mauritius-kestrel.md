Mauritius is the island where the dodo lived and died. By the 1970s it looked as if a small, spotted falcon, the **Mauritius kestrel**, would go the same way.

Its forests had been cut down for centuries. Introduced cats, mongooses and monkeys raided its nests. Then, in the 1950s and 1960s, the pesticide DDT was sprayed widely on the island, poisoning the birds' food. In **1974** only **four** Mauritius kestrels were known in the wild. It was one of the rarest birds on Earth.

Early attempts to breed them in captivity failed. Then a young Welsh biologist, **Carl Jones**, arrived in 1979 and tried everything. He took the first eggs from wild nests so the parents would lay again, hatched the stolen eggs in incubators, left food out for the wild birds, and raised chicks by hand before releasing them.

It worked. Within a few decades there were several hundred kestrels. The story is not finished: numbers have fallen again since their peak, and the bird is still listed as endangered. But a species that was down to four is still flying.

> **Look closer:** At the time, many experts thought the kestrel was doomed. Jones went on to use the same methods to save other Mauritian birds: the pink pigeon, down to about 10 birds in 1990, and the echo parakeet, down to about 10 in the early 1980s.
