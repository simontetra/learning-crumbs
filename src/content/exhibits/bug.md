In **1947**, a team of engineers at **Harvard University** was looking after one of the early computers, the **Mark II**. It was an enormous machine full of switches that clicked open and shut.

One day it went wrong. When they searched inside, they found the cause: a real **moth**, stuck in one of the switches. They carefully took it out and taped it into their logbook, with a joke written beside it: "First actual case of bug being found."

The computer scientist **Grace Hopper** worked on the Mark II and loved telling this story, so it is often said that she found the moth. The Smithsonian, which now looks after the logbook, says the page probably wasn't hers.

And here's the twist. The engineers found it funny because **bug** was *already* the word for a fault. Engineers had been using it for years. **Thomas Edison** wrote about "bugs" in his inventions in the **1870s**. The moth didn't give us the word. It was just the first time a bug really was a bug.

> **Look closer:** Fixing the faults in a computer program is still called *debugging*. Somewhere in a museum, there is a moth taped to a page that makes the joke real.
