Pencil marks could be rubbed away, but with what? For a long time the answer was **bread**. Artists and students rolled soft, slightly damp bread into a ball and dabbed or rubbed it over the paper to lift the graphite. When it got grubby, they broke off a fresh piece.

In **1770** something better came along. The scientist **Joseph Priestley** wrote that a certain substance was "excellently adapted to the purpose of wiping from paper the mark of black-lead-pencil". It was the springy gum from South American trees, and it was being sold for the job in little cubes by **Edward Nairne**, a London maker of scientific instruments.

The new stuff rubbed pencil out so well that in English it became known by what it did: **rubber**. In America it is usually called an eraser.

Early rubber had a problem. It went hard in the cold, sticky in the heat, and slowly rotted. In **1839** Charles Goodyear found that heating rubber with sulphur made it tough and stable, and rubbers soon became cheap and common. In **1858** an American, Hymen Lipman, patented a pencil with a rubber fixed to the end.

> **Look closer:** So the first rubbers were, quite literally, **crumbs**. Some artists still use bread, or a soft putty rubber that works the same way.
