Early bicycle wheels had tyres of solid rubber, or even iron. On cobbled streets, the ride was teeth-rattling.

**John Boyd Dunlop** was a Scottish vet with a busy practice in **Belfast**. In **1887** he wanted to give his young son a smoother ride on his tricycle. So he fitted the wheels with tubes of rubber pumped full of air. The bumps all but disappeared.

Dunlop patented his **pneumatic** (air-filled) tyre in **December 1888**. The next year a cyclist called **Willie Hume** fitted them to his bicycle and won race after race in Belfast, and riders everywhere wanted them.

There was a twist. About two years after the patent was granted, Dunlop was told it was **invalid**. Another Scot, **Robert William Thomson**, had patented an air-filled tyre back in **1846**, for horse-drawn carriages. Thomson's idea had been forgotten, and Dunlop hadn't known about it.

Even so, it was Dunlop's tyre that caught on, just as bicycles, and soon cars, needed it. His name is still on tyres today.

> **Look closer:** Like Brad's Drink before it became Pepsi, the Dunlop company is one of many brands still named after the person who started it.
