Take a **homing pigeon** far from home in a basket, let it go, and it will fly back to its own loft, often from many miles away. Scientists think pigeons use several clues: the position of the **Sun**, the Earth's **magnetic field**, even **smells** and landmarks. Nobody fully understands how they put it all together.

Because they always fly home, pigeons were used to carry **messages** for thousands of years. In the **First World War**, armies carried pigeons to the front line. A soldier would write a note, roll it into a tiny tube on the pigeon's leg, and let it go. The pigeon flew back to its loft behind the lines, and the message was delivered.

The most famous was an American army pigeon called **Cher Ami**, French for **dear friend**. The story goes that on **4 October 1918**, a battalion of American soldiers was trapped in a forest in France and being shelled by its **own side's guns** by mistake. Cher Ami carried a desperate message asking them to stop, flying through gunfire and arriving badly wounded. The shelling stopped, and **194** of the men survived.

Here's the twist. A curator at the **Smithsonian**, where Cher Ami has been on display since 1921, says nothing firmly connects this pigeon to that message; the Army may have mixed up two birds. What's certain is that Cher Ami was a real, much-praised messenger, awarded a French medal for delivering twelve messages in battle.

> **Look closer:** Even museum stories need checking. Asking "how do we know?" can change a story, and that's a good thing.
