In September 1940, an 18-year-old called **Marcel Ravidat** was out near the village of Montignac in south-west France with his dog, **Robot**. A tree had blown down, leaving a hole in the ground, and Robot was very interested in it. Ravidat later liked to say the dog fell in. That part may be a story that grew in the telling.

On **12 September**, Ravidat came back with three friends, **Jacques Marsal**, **Georges Agniel** and **Simon Coencas**. They widened the hole, slid down into the dark, and held up a lamp.

The walls were covered in animals: horses, bulls, deer, a strange spotted creature, painted in reds, yellows and black. There are over 600 paintings and nearly 6,000 figures in all. They were made between about **17,000 and 22,000 years ago**, during the last Ice Age, by people working by the light of flickering lamps.

The boys told their schoolteacher, who called in an expert on prehistory, Abbé Henri Breuil. He called Lascaux "the Sistine Chapel of prehistory".

Visitors poured in, but their breath brought heat, damp and mould that began to harm the paintings. In **1963** the cave was closed to the public. Today people visit full-size copies instead, Lascaux II and Lascaux IV, painted by artists to match the originals.

> **Look closer:** The original cave is still closed. To protect a picture, sometimes the kindest thing is to make a perfect copy and leave the real one alone.
