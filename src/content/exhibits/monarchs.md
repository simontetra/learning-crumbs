Every autumn, **monarch butterflies**, orange and black, flutter south out of Canada and the United States. And every spring they come back. But for a very long time, nobody knew where they went in between.

A Canadian scientist, **Fred Urquhart**, was determined to find out. With his wife **Norah**, he spent more than **twenty years** sticking tiny **labels** on monarchs' wings, each asking whoever found it to send it back to the University of Toronto. Thousands of **volunteers** joined in, tagging butterflies and reporting where they turned up. Bit by bit, the reports traced a path heading south, towards **Mexico**.

In **1972** the Urquharts asked in a newspaper for more help. Two people living in Mexico, Ken and Catalina, answered, and began searching the mountains. On **2 January 1975**, high in a forest of fir trees, they found it: trees so thickly covered with resting monarchs that the branches drooped. There were thought to be about **60 million** butterflies.

Here is the strangest part. A monarch doesn't live long enough to do the whole round trip. It takes **several generations** of butterflies to go north and come back. The ones that fly to Mexico have never been there before, yet they find the same mountain forests their great-great-grandparents left.

> **Look closer:** The answer was found by a team of ordinary people with stickers and notebooks. Science like this is called *citizen science*, and anyone can join in.
