Ordinary steel is strong, but it has a weakness: it **rusts**. Old steel knives went stained and rusty if they weren't dried and polished carefully.

**Harry Brearley** grew up in **Sheffield**, a city famous for steel and for cutlery. He left school at **twelve** to work as a labourer in the steelworks where his father worked, and learned his chemistry on the job. By 1912 he was working in a steel research laboratory.

His job was to find out why the insides of **rifle barrels** wore away so fast, and to make a tougher steel. He tried mixing steel with different amounts of **chromium**. To study each one, he polished a sample and dipped it in acid, which normally eats into steel and shows up its pattern under a microscope.

On **13 August 1913**, he made a steel with about 12.8% chromium that the acid could hardly touch. Brearley thought of Sheffield's knives. If acid couldn't stain it, perhaps vinegar and lemon juice wouldn't either. His employers weren't interested, so he took it to a local cutlery maker, who made some of the first knives from it.

Brearley called it **rustless steel**. The cutlery firm's manager, **Ernest Stuart**, suggested a better name: **stainless steel**.

> **Look closer:** Look at the back of a kitchen knife or a spoon. If it says "stainless", you are holding Harry Brearley's idea.
