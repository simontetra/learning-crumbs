There is no sharp edge where the sky ends. The air gets thinner the higher you go, until it is hard to say where the atmosphere stops and space begins.

Scientists often use the **Kármán line**, at **100 kilometres (62 miles)** above sea level. It is named after Theodore von Kármán, an aerospace engineer who worked out roughly the height at which air can no longer give aircraft enough lift to fly. He rounded the figure to 100 km.

NASA and the US military use about **80 kilometres (50 miles)** instead. The historian Jonathan McDowell also favours the lower figure, which he says is more physically accurate.

> **Look closer:** Above the Kármán line, a spacecraft must go fast enough to stay in orbit, because the air is too thin to hold it up. Below it, an aircraft flies on the air itself.
