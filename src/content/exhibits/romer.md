Switch on a light and the room fills with light *instantly*. Or does it? For most of history, people believed light simply arrived everywhere at once.

In the 1670s, a young Danish astronomer called **Ole Rømer** was working at the **Paris Observatory**, timing **Io**, one of the moons of Jupiter. Io goes round Jupiter like clockwork, disappearing into Jupiter's shadow at regular times. Astronomers used those eclipses as a kind of clock in the sky.

But the clock didn't keep perfect time. When the Earth was moving **away** from Jupiter, the eclipses seemed to come later and later. When the Earth was moving **towards** Jupiter, they came early.

Rømer realised that the moon wasn't misbehaving. **The light was.** When Earth is further from Jupiter, the light showing us Io has further to travel, so we see each eclipse a little later. In **1676** he predicted that one eclipse would appear about **ten minutes** later than expected.

He worked out that light takes about **22 minutes** to cross the width of the Earth's orbit. Many people didn't believe him. His number wasn't perfect, about a quarter too slow, but he had shown something nobody had proved before: light has a **speed**.

> **Look closer:** Light really takes about 8 minutes to reach us from the Sun. So when you see the Sun, you're seeing it as it was eight minutes ago.
