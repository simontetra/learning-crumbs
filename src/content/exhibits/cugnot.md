Long before petrol, before bicycles, before railways, a French army engineer called **Nicolas-Joseph Cugnot** had an idea: what if a machine could do the work of horses?

The French army used teams of four horses to drag its heavy cannons. Around **1770**, Cugnot built a **fardier à vapeur**, a steam wagon, to do the job instead. It had **three wheels**, with a huge round boiler hanging off the front, and it steered by turning the single front wheel.

It worked, after a fashion. It could carry a heavy load about as fast as soldiers could march, which isn't very fast at all.

There is a famous story that, during one trial, the wagon ran out of control and **knocked down a wall**. If it's true, it was the world's first motor accident. Historians aren't sure it happened, though they have found that the boiler on the surviving wagon isn't the original, which hints that something may have been damaged.

The army lost interest, and Cugnot's idea was put away. But the wagon itself survived. You can still see it today in a museum in **Paris**, the Conservatoire national des arts et métiers.

> **Look closer:** So the first vehicle that moved itself along a road ran on steam, not petrol, and was built for war, not for families.
