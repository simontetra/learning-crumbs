The Pacific Ocean is enormous, dotted with tiny islands thousands of miles apart. Yet people reached and settled almost all of them, long before anyone had a compass or a map. Some historians thought they must have drifted there by accident. Others believed they had sailed on purpose. How could anyone prove it?

In the 1970s, a group in **Hawaii** built a traditional double-hulled voyaging canoe, **Hōkūleʻa**. To sail it the old way, they needed a navigator who still knew how. They found **Mau Piailug**, a master navigator from **Satawal**, a tiny island in Micronesia, who had learned the ancient art as a boy.

In **1976**, Mau guided Hōkūleʻa from Hawaii to **Tahiti** with **no instruments at all**. He kept a "star compass" in his head, knowing where each star rises and sets on the horizon. By day he read the **sun**, the **winds** and the **clouds**. He felt the long ocean **swells** through the hull of the canoe. Near the end, birds such as white terns, which fly out to sea to fish and back to land at night, told him an island was near. After **31 days**, they arrived.

The voyage showed that the ancient Pacific navigators could have travelled on purpose, in both directions. It also brought the art back to life. Mau went on to teach a young Hawaiian, **Nainoa Thompson**, who navigated Hōkūleʻa to Tahiti and back in 1980.

> **Look closer:** Like the dung beetle, Mau read the stars; like the salmon and the turtle, he read the ocean itself. People, it turns out, can be born navigators too, if someone teaches them.
