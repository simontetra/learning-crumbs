The deepest holes ever drilled barely scratch the surface of the Earth. Nobody has ever been anywhere near the middle. So how do we know what's down there?

The answer is **earthquakes**. When a big earthquake happens, it sends waves through the whole planet, and instruments called **seismographs** all over the world record when they arrive. The waves travel at different speeds through different materials, and they bend when they pass from one layer to another. So by carefully comparing the timings from many places, scientists can work out what the waves must have passed through.

**Inge Lehmann**, a Danish seismologist born in 1888, was studying the records from a large earthquake in **New Zealand** in **1929**. Scientists already knew the Earth had a liquid core, which should bend the waves away and leave a "shadow zone" where none arrived. But some faint waves were turning up in the shadow zone anyway.

Lehmann worked out the explanation: inside the liquid core there must be a **solid inner core**, bouncing some of the waves into places they otherwise couldn't reach. She published the idea in **1936**, in a paper with one of the shortest titles in science: **P′**.

> **Look closer:** She is said to have sorted her earthquake records on cards kept in oatmeal boxes. She lived to be 105.
