When a baby **loggerhead turtle** hatches, it digs its way out of the sand and scurries down the beach into the sea. Then it vanishes, often for many years, sometimes swimming right across an ocean.

When a female is grown up and ready to lay her eggs, she comes back to nest **on the same stretch of beach where she was born**. How does she find it?

Scientists at the **University of North Carolina** had an idea: the turtles might remember the **magnetic signature** of their home beach, just as salmon seem to remember their river. To test it, a team led by **J. Roger Brothers** studied **19 years** of nesting records along the east coast of **Florida**.

Here's the clever part. The Earth's magnetic field slowly drifts, because the molten metal deep inside the planet is always moving. Where the magnetic signatures of neighbouring beaches drifted **closer together**, the turtles' nests crowded together too. Where they drifted **apart**, the nests spread out. The turtles seemed to be following the magnetic map, wherever it moved.

Nobody yet knows exactly how turtles sense magnetism. One idea is tiny magnetic particles in their bodies.

> **Look closer:** It's a lovely bit of detective work. The scientists never had to follow a single turtle; the answer was hidden in nineteen years of ordinary nesting records.
