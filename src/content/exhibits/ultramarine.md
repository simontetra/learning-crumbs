Cave painters had plenty of reds, yellows and blacks, made from coloured earth and charcoal. A strong, bright **blue** was much harder to find.

The best blue of all came from **lapis lazuli**, a deep blue stone mined in the mountains of **Afghanistan**. Painters ground it into powder and carefully washed out the best of the colour. The finished paint was called **ultramarine**, from the Latin for "beyond the sea", because it had travelled such a long way to reach Europe.

It was so rare that the best quality could be worth **more than gold**. People who paid for paintings sometimes said exactly how much ultramarine they wanted used. Painters saved it for the most important parts of a picture, such as the robes of the Virgin Mary.

In the 1820s a prize was offered in France for anyone who could make ultramarine artificially. In **1828** a chemist called **Jean-Baptiste Guimet** won it, cooking up the colour from clay, soda, charcoal, quartz and sulphur. Suddenly the most precious blue in the world was cheap enough for anyone.

> **Look closer:** Guimet's kind of ultramarine is still one of the most common blues in paint today, often sold as "French ultramarine".
