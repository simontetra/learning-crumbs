A young salmon hatches in a small stream, swims down to the sea, and disappears into the ocean. It may roam **thousands of miles** for years. Then, when it is ready to lay its own eggs, it does something astonishing: it comes back, not just to the right coast, but to the very **stream where it was born**.

How?

For the long journey across the open ocean, scientists think the answer is **magnetism**. The Earth is a giant magnet, and its magnetic field is slightly different from place to place, a bit like an invisible address. The idea is that when young salmon leave their river, they **remember the magnetic address** of its mouth. Years later, they swim until the field feels the same again.

In **2013**, a team led by **Nathan Putman** of Oregon State University reported the first real evidence for this, from records of salmon returning to their rivers. When the Earth's magnetic field shifted slightly over the years, the salmon's routes home seemed to shift with it.

Once they reach the coast, salmon switch to another sense: **smell**. Every stream has its own mix of smells from the soil, rocks and plants around it, and the salmon seem to remember the smell of home. They follow it upstream, leaping waterfalls on the way, until they arrive.

> **Look closer:** Salmon raised in hatcheries may struggle to learn their magnetic address. Metal in tanks and electrical cables can confuse the weak magnetic field.
