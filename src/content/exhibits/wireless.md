A hundred and fifty years ago, if you wanted to send a message a long way fast, it went down a **wire**: the telegraph, and later the telephone. Wires were strung on poles across countries and laid along the bottom of the sea.

A young Italian inventor, **Guglielmo Marconi**, wanted to send messages through the air instead, using invisible **radio waves**. People called it **wireless**, because that was exactly what was new about it.

On **12 December 1901**, Marconi sat in a building on **Signal Hill** in Newfoundland, Canada. His aerial was a long wire held up by a **kite**. Across the Atlantic, a station at **Poldhu** in Cornwall was tapping out the letter **S** in Morse code: three short dots. Pressing an earpiece to his ear, Marconi heard the faint *dot dot dot*. A signal had crossed the ocean with no wire at all.

At first wireless carried only dots and dashes. Within a few years, inventors learned to send **voices and music**. On **14 November 1922**, at 6 o'clock in the evening, the new **British Broadcasting Company**, the BBC, began daily broadcasts from its London station, **2LO**. Arthur Burrows read the news twice, once fast and once slowly, and asked listeners which speed they preferred.

For the next fifty years, many British families called the radio set in the corner of the room **the wireless**.

> **Look closer:** Your phone, your games controller and your Bluetooth headphones are all wireless too, sending messages on radio waves, just like Marconi's.
