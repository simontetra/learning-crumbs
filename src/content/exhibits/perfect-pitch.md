Most people can tell whether one note is higher or lower than another. But if you play a single note on a piano and ask which one it was, almost everyone has to guess.

A few people don't need to guess. They hear a note, a car horn, even the hum of a fridge, and simply **know** what note it is, the way you know a colour is red. This is called **absolute pitch**, or **perfect pitch**.

It is rare. In the United States and Europe, scientists estimate that **fewer than 1 in 10,000** people have it.

Nobody fully understands why some people do. One clue came from the psychologist **Diana Deutsch**. Her team tested music students in **Beijing** and in **Rochester**, New York. Among students who had started lessons at four or five, about **60 in every 100** of the Chinese students had perfect pitch, compared with about **14 in 100** of the Americans.

The Chinese students all spoke **Mandarin**, a **tone language**, where the pitch of your voice changes what a word means. Deutsch thinks learning a tone language as a baby, and starting music young, may both help the brain hold on to exact pitches.

> **Look closer:** In Deutsch's study, boys and girls were just as likely as each other to have perfect pitch. What seemed to matter was language, and how young you started.
