**Eratosthenes** was the chief librarian of the great Library of **Alexandria**, in Egypt, more than 2,200 years ago. Educated Greeks already knew the Earth was round. But how big was it? Nobody could walk all the way round to find out.

Eratosthenes heard about a deep well far to the south, at **Syene**. On one day each year, at noon, the sun shone straight down to the bottom of it, lighting the water. That meant the sun was **directly overhead** there.

At the same moment, in Alexandria, an upright post still cast a small **shadow**. The sun wasn't quite overhead there. Eratosthenes measured the angle: about **7.2 degrees**.

Here's the clever bit. Because the Earth is round, that small angle is the same as the angle between the two cities, measured from the centre of the Earth. And **7.2 degrees is one fiftieth of a full circle**. So the distance between the two cities must be one fiftieth of the way around the whole world.

The cities were about **5,000 stadia** apart. Fifty times that is **250,000 stadia**. Nobody is sure exactly how long his stadium was, but by one common estimate his answer was only about 16% too big, from a well, a shadow and a bit of geometry.

> **Look closer:** You can try it yourself: two people in different towns, a stick each, measuring their shadows at the same moment, just as Eratosthenes did.
