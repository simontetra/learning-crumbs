By the 1960s you could record sound on **magnetic tape**, but tape came on big open reels. You had to thread it through the machine by hand, and it tangled easily.

**Lou Ottens**, an engineer leading new products for the Dutch company **Philips**, wanted something anyone could use. Before designing anything, he carved a small, thin **block of wood**, the size he wanted the new tape to be. It fitted in his **jacket pocket**.

The result, launched in **1963**, was the **compact cassette**: a little plastic case with both reels of tape sealed inside. You just pushed it into the machine. It was small, cheap and hard to break, and you could **record** onto it yourself, from the radio or a microphone.

Ottens persuaded Philips to let other companies make cassettes for **free**, so the design became the same all over the world. According to the Dutch newspaper NRC, around **100 billion** cassettes have been sold.

> **Look closer:** The wooden block didn't survive. Ottens later used it to prop up a car jack while changing a tyre, and it was lost. Nearly twenty years after the cassette, he also helped bring us the CD.
