Here's the puzzle. If you have always seen traffic lights a certain way, and everyone has always told you the top one is called **red**, you'll call it red too. You learned the name and the colour together. So how could you ever find out that your red isn't the same as everyone else's?

Usually, the answer is: **by accident, when you compare notes with other people.**

That is what happened to the scientist **John Dalton**. In **1792** he noticed that a geranium flower looked blue in daylight but quite a different colour by candlelight. To him, the change was obvious. His friends saw no change at all. He had already noticed that fellow Quakers thought the clothes he wore were too bright, when to him they looked quiet and plain. Putting the clues together, he realised his eyes worked differently. His brother's did too. In **1794** he described it to scientists in Manchester. In French, colour blindness is still called *daltonisme*.

Today we don't wait for a lucky flower. In **1917** a Japanese professor, **Shinobu Ishihara**, published a book of circles made of coloured dots. Hidden in the dots is a number. Most people see it at once. People with red-green colour blindness see a different number, or none.

It's common. About **1 in 12 men** and **1 in 200 women** have some colour blindness. And traffic lights? Many colour-blind drivers simply go by position: red is always at the top.

> **Look closer:** Dalton thought the fluid in his eyes must be tinted blue, and asked for his eyes to be examined after he died. They weren't tinted. In 1995, scientists tested DNA from his preserved eyes and found the real answer: he was missing the genes for one of the three colour detectors, the one for green.
