A **dung beetle** has one big job: find a fresh pile of dung, shape a piece into a ball, and roll it away to eat later. It has to get away fast, in a **straight line**, because other beetles are waiting to steal it. If it went round in circles, it could end up back at the pile, and lose its ball.

Some dung beetles in **South Africa** do this at **night**. How do they keep straight in the dark?

Scientists led by **Marie Dacke** and **Eric Warrant** from Lund University in Sweden suspected the sky. So they tried giving some beetles **tiny cardboard hats** that blocked their view upwards. Those beetles wandered about aimlessly. Beetles wearing clear hats kept rolling straight.

Then the team took their beetles to the **Johannesburg Planetarium**, where they could switch the stars on and off. Under a full starry sky, the beetles rolled straight. Under just the faint band of the **Milky Way**, they still rolled straight. With no stars at all, they got lost.

In **2013** they announced that dung beetles are the first animals known to steer by the **Milky Way**.

> **Look closer:** A dung beetle's eyes are far too small to pick out single stars. But they can see the soft glowing stripe of our galaxy across the sky, and that's enough.
