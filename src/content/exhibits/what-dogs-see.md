You may have heard that dogs see the world in **black and white**. It was written in a dog-training book back in **1937**, and people repeated it for decades. It's wrong. But how could anyone find out? You can't ask a dog.

The scientist **Jay Neitz** came up with a game. A dog was shown **three panels of light** in a row. Two were the same colour; one was different. If the dog pressed its nose on the odd one out, a treat dropped into a cup underneath. Dogs are very good at learning games for treats.

By changing the colours again and again, the scientists could see which colours the dogs could tell apart, and which ones looked the same to them. The answer: dogs **do** see colour, but far fewer colours than we do. They tell **blues** and **yellows** apart well, but **reds and greens** look much the same, a sort of greyish brown. A dog's eye has two kinds of colour detector, where ours has three, so its view of the world is a lot like that of a person with red-green colour blindness.

> **Look closer:** Bulls can't see red either. In bullfights, it's the movement of the cape that makes them charge. When the TV show *MythBusters* tested it in 2007, a bull ignored a person standing still in red and chased people who were moving.
