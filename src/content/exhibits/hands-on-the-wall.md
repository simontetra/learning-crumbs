One of the oldest ways of making a picture needs no brush at all. You press your hand flat against a wall, take a mouthful of coloured paint, and blow it in a fine spray around your fingers. Take your hand away and its outline stays behind. This is a **hand stencil**.

People have made hand stencils on cave walls all over the world. In January 2026, a team of Indonesian and Australian scientists reported the oldest one yet, in a limestone cave called **Liang Metanduno** on **Muna Island**, in Sulawesi, Indonesia.

To date it, they measured tiny crusts of mineral, nicknamed "cave popcorn", that had slowly grown over the paint. The paint must be older than whatever grew on top of it. The answer: **at least 67,800 years old**.

Whoever made it did something curious. The fingers of the stencil were narrowed, perhaps by moving the hand, to look pointed, almost like claws. So far, that style has only been found in Sulawesi.

Scientists still debate exactly which early humans made it. But the picture tells us something without words: long before writing, someone wanted to say *I was here*.

> **Look closer:** Because the island lies on the way to New Guinea and Australia, the stencil also helps show when people were making the long sea crossings to get there.
